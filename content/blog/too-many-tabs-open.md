Some people know what they want to be early on, and then they go and become it. Then there are the rest of us, with thirty tabs open at once: a half-read book on typography, a guitar that has slowly become furniture, a language app still sending hopeful reminders, a side project, a recipe we're definitely going to master, and a vague plan to finally understand how planes stay up.

If that sounds like you, you've probably been told some version of "you need to focus". It's usually said kindly. It still lands like a diagnosis.

I don't think it is one. Having many interests is a way of being curious about the world, and the world is very large. It does need handling, though. Left alone, it turns into a browser that freezes every time you try to open something new.

## the guilt of not choosing

The hardest part of having many interests is usually the guilt, more than the time.

Every hour spent on one thing starts to feel like a betrayal of the others. You sit down to draw and think about the code you didn't write. You write the code and think about the drawing. You end up half-present everywhere, which is more tiring than doing any one thing properly.

The way out starts with a simple permission: you don't have to choose forever. You only have to choose for now.

## one main thing, a few on the side

What works for a lot of people is a loose ranking that changes with the seasons.

- One main thing gets your best hours, most days. For a few months, it's the answer when someone asks what you're working on.
- Two or three side things get the leftovers: a weekend morning, a train ride, a slow evening. No deadlines and no guilt.
- Everything else gets parked on a written list, so you can stop carrying it around in your head.

Every few months (not every few days), look at the ranking again. Promote something that's been pulling at you. Let something else rest. The list is how you stay curious without becoming scattered.

## let your interests talk to each other

People who tell you to focus tend to miss one thing: the overlap between your interests is often where your best work comes from.

A designer who understands code builds things that actually ship. A programmer who plays music can hear the rhythm in a good interface. A cook who reads about chemistry stops following recipes and starts understanding them. One deep interest can make you excellent. A few connected ones can lead you to things nobody else would think to make.

So pay attention when two of your tabs start talking to each other. That's usually the moment to lean in.

## finish small things

The real risk with many interests is starting everything and finishing nothing, until "I'm into lots of things" quietly turns into "I never complete anything", and your confidence takes the hit.

The fix is to move the finish line closer. Instead of "learn the guitar", play one song all the way through. Instead of "build an app", ship one page that works. Small finishes give you proof that you follow through, and that proof makes the next start far easier.

## it's fine to close a tab

Some interests simply run their course. You loved something for a year and now you don't, and that's allowed. The year still counts. You kept whatever it taught you, even if you never pick it up again.

Closing a tab you've outgrown frees up memory for the next thing that catches your eye. That's how a curious life is supposed to work.

## the person with many tabs open

There's a quiet pressure to be one thing, so people can file you away easily. Engineer, designer, writer: pick a box and stay in it.

You don't have to. You can be the person who knows a little too much about fonts, cooks on Sundays, reads about space and builds things for a living. All you need is a system kind enough to hold every one of those tabs, and honest enough to tell you which one to work in today.
